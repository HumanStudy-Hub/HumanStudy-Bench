# Paper context

## Overview

`o-overview`

- **Method type**: Between-subjects survey experiment (four conditions, block-randomized) fielded online by YouGov (p. 4, p. 3)
- **Summary**: Respondents read a sequence of news dispatches about a fictional Alaska state senator's sudden resignation. Conditions differed in whether the resignation was left unexplained (control), linked by innuendo to a bribery investigation, followed by a denial backed by a prosecutors' letter, or followed by the denial plus an alternate causal explanation (a new job). After a delay task, respondents rated the senator's favorability and the likelihood of bribery or illegal practices (and, outside the control condition, of resigning because of an investigation). (p. 1, p. 3, p. 4)

## Research question and background

`b-bg`

- **Question**: Whether a causal explanation for an unexplained event corrects a false (innuendo) account better than a denial alone, for new political misperceptions about a breaking news event. (p. 1, p. 3)
- **Gap**: Prior political-misinformation work mostly examines misperceptions already widely held; this study examines new misperceptions as they form, in a fast-breaking political news story with speculative and unconfirmed reports. (p. 3, p. 2)
- **Theory**: People automatically make causal inferences from the information at hand; false information can persist after being debunked unless it is displaced by an alternate causal account (continued influence effect). (p. 2, p. 1)
- **Concepts**: Continued influence effect, belief perseverance, innuendo, denial, causal correction. In the canonical paradigm (Wilkes and Leatherbarrow 1988) subjects are told one piece of information at a time about an unfolding fictional event; Johnson and Seifert (1994) showed the false link can be displaced by an alternative cause. (p. 2)
