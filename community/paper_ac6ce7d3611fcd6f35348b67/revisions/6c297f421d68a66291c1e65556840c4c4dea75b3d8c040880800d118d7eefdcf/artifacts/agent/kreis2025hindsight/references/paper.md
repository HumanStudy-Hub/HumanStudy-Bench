# Paper context

## Overview

`n_overview`

- **Method type**: Preregistered online between-groups experiment with a within-person task factor (conceptual replication of Groß et al., 2023, Exp. 2) (p. 2, p. 4, p. 11)
- **Summary**: Participants estimate the sugar content of 32 food items (original judgments, OJ), then see numerical information that differs by group (actual values for the same items, values for other food items, the same values relabeled as longitudes of European cities, or an unrelated text), then recall their original estimates (ROJ) and estimate 32 new food items (NJ). Hindsight effects (ROJ closer to the actual values than OJ) and transfer learning effects (NJ closer than OJ) are compared across groups. (p. 6, p. 11)

## Research question and rationale

`n_background`

- **Question**: Does the link between hindsight bias and knowledge updating found by Groß et al. (2023) for country populations generalize to a domain people know better, the sugar content of food items? (p. 2, p. 4)
- **Gap**: It is unclear whether hindsight bias and knowledge updating are equally influenced by familiarity and whether familiarity changes the association between them; the original domain (country populations, numbers in the millions) is unfamiliar. (p. 4)
- **Theory**: Knowledge-updating account: hindsight bias is a by-product of adaptive updating of the knowledge base; recalling an initial judgment then means re-judging the object with updated knowledge. The traditional view is anchoring and adjustment (cognitive error). (p. 3)
- **Key concepts**: Hindsight effect: |Δ| smaller for ROJs than OJs. Transfer learning effect: |Δ| smaller for NJs than OJs. Seed facts: actual values presented for a set of objects. Three derived predictions: transfer learning for the whole domain, hindsight via domain information, no hindsight via irrelevant information. (p. 5, p. 6, p. 7)
