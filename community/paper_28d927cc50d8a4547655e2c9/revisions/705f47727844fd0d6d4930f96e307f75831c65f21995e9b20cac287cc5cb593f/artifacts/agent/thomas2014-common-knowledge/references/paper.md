# Paper context

## Paper overview

`ov`

- **Year**: 2014 (p. 3)
- **Method type**: Three between-subjects online experiments (Amazon Mechanical Turk) with a role-played Stag Hunt coordination game and incentivised decisions (p. 12, p. 17)
- **Summary**: Participants chose between working alone for a sure profit and working together for a slightly higher profit that is earned only if the partner(s) also choose to work together. What each participant was told about the partner's knowledge of the payoff (primary, secondary, tertiary or common knowledge) and the payoff size were manipulated. Reported: coordination attempts were higher with common knowledge than with tertiary (shared) knowledge in every payoff condition of all three experiments. (p. 3, p. 31)

## Question, gap and theory

`bg`

- **Research question**: Whether people are sensitive to common knowledge, as distinct from shared (finite-level) knowledge and primary knowledge, when deciding whether to engage in risky coordination. (p. 3, p. 12)
- **Gap**: Research on cooperation has concentrated on altruism; the complementary problem of mutualism and the psychology of coordination is little studied. (p. 3, p. 5)
- **Theory**: Game-theoretic models of coordination (Stag Hunt) imply common knowledge has a privileged role in enabling coordination; shared knowledge falls short of infinite levels of embedded knowledge and primary knowledge is knowledge without knowing whether others have it. (p. 7, p. 5)
- **Key concepts**: Primary knowledge, shared knowledge (finite levels, here secondary and tertiary), common knowledge (broadcast publicly). (p. 7)

## Overall conclusion across experiments

`find_all`

- **Answer**: In all three experiments, with every payoff combination, participants were more likely to attempt risky coordination with common knowledge than shared knowledge, and little distinction was made between levels of shared knowledge. (p. 31)
- **Support**: Coordination attempts with common knowledge did not closely track the cost-benefit ratio, indicating a categorical recognition of common knowledge. (p. 31)
- **Contribution**: Common knowledge is represented as a distinct conceptual category. (p. 31)

## Validity and scope as discussed by the authors

`lim_all`

- **Validity**: The authors note the similarity manipulation may have been too cursory to be effective and that rewards were abstract monetary payoffs differing only in magnitude, not in semantic content. (p. 32)
- **Scope**: Authors describe the game as an artificial, contrived scenario of interacting with strangers on the Internet in which predicting whether coordination is profitable is very hard. (p. 33)
- **Limitations**: Scenario is role-played with small payoffs chosen to counter demand characteristics for cooperative actions; the quantity of money at stake is a few dollars at most. (p. 18)
- **Replications**: The paper reports no independent replication; Experiment 3 replicates the $2 payoff condition of Experiment 1 with added components. (p. 26)
- **Openness**: Not stated in the paper.
