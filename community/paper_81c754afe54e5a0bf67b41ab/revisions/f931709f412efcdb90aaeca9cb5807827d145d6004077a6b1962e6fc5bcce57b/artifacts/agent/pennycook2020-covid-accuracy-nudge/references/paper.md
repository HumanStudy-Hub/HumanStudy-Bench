# Paper context

## Overview

`n_overview`

- **Year**: 2020 (p. 1)
- **Method type [derived]**: Two online survey experiments with U.S. adults (Lucid quota sample), randomised between participants, with true and false COVID-19 news headlines as repeated stimuli, plus a post hoc item-level analysis combining both studies. (p. 1, p. 3, p. 6)
  - how it was derived: Combined from the abstract and the Study 1 and Study 2 method sections.
- **Summary [derived]**: Study 1 compares accuracy judgments with sharing intentions for 15 false and 15 true COVID-19 headlines and relates truth discernment to individual differences. Study 2 tests whether first rating the accuracy of one unrelated headline raises truth discernment in later sharing intentions. (p. 1, p. 2)
  - how it was derived: Condensed from the abstract and the statements of aim for each study.

## Research question and inattention account

`n_background`

- **Question [derived]**: Do people share false COVID-19 claims partly because they fail to consider accuracy when deciding what to share, and does a subtle accuracy reminder improve what they are willing to share? (p. 1, p. 2)
  - how it was derived: Rephrased from the abstract and the aims of Studies 1 and 2.
- **Gap** (verbatim, p. 2):
  > It was unclear, however, how (or whether) these results would generalize to COVID-19.
- **Theory** (verbatim, p. 2):
  > According to this account, people generally wish to avoid spreading misinformation and, in fact, are often able to tell truth from falsehood; however, they nonetheless share false and misleading content because the social media context focuses their attention on factors other than accuracy
- **Concepts**: Truth discernment: the difference in accuracy judgments (or sharing intentions) between true and false headlines. (p. 3)

## Validity, scope and openness

`n_limits`

- **Validity**: Self-reported sharing is supported by evidence that headlines with higher reported sharing likelihood receive more shares on Twitter; the subtle manipulation makes demand effects unlikely. (p. 3, p. 6)
- **Scope**: Evidence is restricted to the United States; the quota sample is not probability based and should not be considered truly nationally representative. (p. 9)
- **Limitations**: A particular set of true and false COVID-19 headlines; sharing intentions were hypothetical and the accuracy induction was performed in a 'lab' context. (p. 9)
- **Replications reported**: Pennycook et al. (2020) applied the same accuracy-reminder intervention to political misinformation and replicated the survey results with direct messages on Twitter. (p. 9)
- **Openness**: Data and materials are on OSF (https://osf.io/7d3xh/); Studies 1 and 2 were preregistered at AsPredicted; the preregistration erroneously planned to cluster standard errors on participant only. (p. 9, p. 10)
