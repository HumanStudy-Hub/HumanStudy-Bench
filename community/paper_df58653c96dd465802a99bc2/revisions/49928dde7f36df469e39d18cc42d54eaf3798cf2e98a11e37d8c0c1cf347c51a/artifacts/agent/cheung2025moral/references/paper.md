# Paper context

## Overview

`ov`

Cheung, Maier & Lieder: LLMs versus people in moral dilemmas and collective action problems.

- **Method type [to check]**: Online survey experiments with human participants (Prolific), compared with repeated single-turn queries of large language models. (p. 1, p. 5)
- **Summary [to check]**: Four studies compare LLM moral decisions and advice with those of U.S. samples. LLMs were more altruistic in collective action problems and showed a stronger omission bias and, unlike people, a yes-no bias in moral dilemmas (Studies 1-3); Study 4 compares Llama 3.1 with and without fine-tuning and Centaur. (p. 1)
- **Ethics approval [to check]**: All experiments received ethical approval from the UCLA Office of the Human Research Protection Program, protocol IRB#23-001436; informed consent was obtained from all participants. (p. 19)

## Research question and background

`bg`

- **Question [to check]**: Are AI systems, such as chatbots, able to make sound moral judgments and decisions? The paper asks how well LLMs emulate or advise on people's decisions in realistic moral dilemmas and collective action problems. (p. 2, p. 5)
- **Gap [to check]**: Prior work mostly used trolley problems, in which action usually coincides with the cost-benefit (CBR) option and omission with the rule option, which confounds action/omission with the moral view. (p. 3)
- **Key concepts [to check]**: CBR option = (naive) cost-benefit reasoning choice of committing sacrificial harm or breaking a rule for the greater good; rule option = the choice that follows (or does not violate) a moral rule. Collective action problems pit self-interest against the greater good. (p. 3, p. 4)

## Findings

`fin`

- **Answer [to check]**: LLMs showed a stronger omission bias than humans in moral dilemmas, were sensitive to whether endorsing the choice coincided with answering "yes" or "no" (a yes-no bias not shown by people), and were more altruistic than people in collective action problems. (p. 13, p. 14)
- **Support [to check]**: Study 2 and Study 3 reframing results; Study 1 framing and altruism results. (p. 9, p. 12)
- **Source of the biases [to check]**: Study 4 suggests the amplified omission bias and the yes-no bias of Llama 3.1-Instruct arose from fine-tuning for chatbot use rather than from pre-training or architecture; the authors speculate the same for the other chatbots. (p. 16, p. 18)
- **Implication [to check]**: Moral decisions and advice of LLMs are more biased and inconsistent than people's, so we should be reluctant to outsource moral decisions to them. (p. 15)

## Limitations

`lim`

- **Sampling [to check]**: Samples deviate from the general population and from people who query LLMs; representative sampling was used in Studies 1 and 3. (p. 17)
- **Mechanism [to check]**: It remains unclear which component of fine-tuning caused the biases; the amplified omission and yes-no bias could in principle arise from architecture, training data or fine-tuning. (p. 18, p. 16)
- **Openness [to check]**: Materials are at the OSF repositories named in the paper; preregistrations for Studies 2 and 3. The SI Appendix is said to be "online (TBA)" in the Methods. (p. 19, p. 7, p. 11)
