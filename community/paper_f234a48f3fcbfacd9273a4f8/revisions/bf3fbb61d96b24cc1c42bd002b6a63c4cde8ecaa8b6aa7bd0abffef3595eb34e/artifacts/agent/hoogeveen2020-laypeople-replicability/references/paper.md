# Paper context

## Overview

`n-overview`

- **Year**: 2020 (p. 1)
- **Method type**: Online between-participant prediction survey (preregistered) with Bayesian analyses (p. 1, p. 2)
- **Summary**: 233 laypeople (no Ph.D. in psychology) judged, for each of 27 high-profile social-science findings, whether it would replicate successfully and how confident they were. Half saw only a plain-language description; half also saw the original study's Bayes factor. Accuracy was 59% (description only) and 67% (description plus evidence). (p. 1)

## Research question

`n-bg`

- **Question**: Can people without a Ph.D. in psychology or related professional background, and without the statistical evidence from the original study, predict whether a finding will replicate? Does adding the strength of original evidence (Bayes factor) improve predictions? (p. 2)
- **Gap**: Experts forecast replication outcomes above chance, but those forecasters had a social-science Ph.D. and some were given p values, effect sizes and sample sizes; it was open whether expertise or statistical information is needed. (p. 2)

## Findings

`f1`

- **Answer**: Laypeople predicted replication outcomes above chance with only descriptions (59%), and better with the Bayes factor (67%); confidence tracked replication effect size. (p. 13)
- **Mechanism proposed**: Intuitions about plausibility carry information about replicability (p. 13)

## Validity and scope

`lim1`

- **Scope**: Expected to generalize to most people given plain-language materials; limited to studies comprehensible to laypeople (p. 14)
- **Limitations**: Bayes factors may act as demand characteristics; replication outcomes are not the truth and depend on the success criterion; the BF explanation in the survey misstated Bayes rule; Dutch participants' order not randomized (p. 15, p. 17)
- **Openness**: Preregistered; materials and data on OSF (https://osf.io/wg9s3) (p. 3)
