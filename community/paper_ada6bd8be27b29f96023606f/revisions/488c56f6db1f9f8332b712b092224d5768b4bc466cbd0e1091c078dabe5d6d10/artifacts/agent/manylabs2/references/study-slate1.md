# Many Labs 2, Slate 1 (13 effects, 64 samples)

`slate1`

One survey script administering the thirteen Slate 1 effects in random order, followed by individual difference measures, data quality items and demographics.

## Hypotheses

### Hypothesis: Direction and socioeconomic status

`h-huan`

- **Statement**: People place a high-SES person further toward the cardinal direction where wealth is concentrated in their own experience: U.S. participants expect the high-SES person to live further north than the low-SES person, Hong Kong participants the reverse. (p. 22, p. 23)

### Hypothesis: Structure and goal pursuit

`h-kay`

- **Statement**: Willingness to pursue goals is higher after exposure to structured versus random events. (p. 25)

### Hypothesis: Disfluency engages analytical processing

`h-alt`

- **Statement**: Syllogism performance is higher when the font is harder to read versus easier to read (incidental disfluency activates analytic processing). (p. 26, p. 27)

### Hypothesis: Moral foundations

`h-grah`

- **Statement**: "Binding" foundations (ingroup, authority, purity) are perceived as more morally relevant by members of the political right than the political left. (p. 28)

### Hypothesis: Affect and risk

`h-rott`

- **Statement**: Affectively attractive choices are more preferred when they are uncertain versus definite. (p. 29)

### Hypothesis: Priming consumerism

`h-bau`

- **Statement**: Trust is lower when thinking of others as consumers versus thinking of others as individuals. (p. 30)

### Hypothesis: Correspondence bias

`h-miya`

- **Statement**: Perceivers ascribe to an actor an attitude corresponding to the actor's assigned behaviour (correspondence bias), more strongly among Americans than Japanese. (p. 31, p. 32)

### Hypothesis: Disgust predicts homophobia

`h-inba`

- **Statement**: Disgust sensitivity and judged intentionality are more strongly related when the director encourages gay kissing than kissing. (p. 33)

### Hypothesis: Incidental anchors

`h-crit`

- **Statement**: Sales estimates are influenced by incidental anchors in the environment (a high versus low number in the product name). (p. 34, p. 35)

### Hypothesis: Social value orientations

`h-van`

- **Statement**: More siblings is positively related with prosocial orientation (sibling-prosocial hypothesis). (p. 36)

### Hypothesis: Trolley dilemma 1

`h-haus12`

- **Statement**: Harm is judged more permissible as a foreseen side effect than as the means to a greater good (principle of double effect). (p. 37)

### Hypothesis: Sociometric status and well-being

`h-and`

- **Statement**: Subjective well-being is higher for participants exposed to higher versus lower sociometric status descriptions. (p. 38)

### Hypothesis: False consensus: supermarket

`h-ross1`

- **Statement**: Participants' choices are positively related to their estimate of the percentage of others who would make the same choice (supermarket vignette). (p. 39)

## Procedure

Order of steps:

- Informed consent (p. 16)
- Effects of the slate in random order per participant (p. 16, p. 17)
  - **Order rule**: Effects are presented in a fully randomized order at the level of participants (p. 17)
  - Effect 1: Direction and socioeconomic status (participant sees: Direction and socioeconomic status, High-SES target (Dr. Bennett, wealthy businessman): printed items; Direction and socioeconomic status, Low-SES target (Mr. Bennett, unemployed): printed items) (p. 22, p. 23)
  - Effect 2: Structure and goal pursuit (participant sees: Structure and goal pursuit, Structured event (leaves grow in orderly patterns): printed items; Structure and goal pursuit, Random event (leaves grow in random patterns): printed items) (p. 25)
  - Effect 3: Disfluency engages analytical processing (participant sees: Disfluency engages analytical processing, Easy-to-read font (black Myriad Web 12-point): printed items; Disfluency engages analytical processing, Hard-to-read font (10% grey italicized Myriad Web 10-point): printed items) (p. 26)
  - Effect 4: Moral foundations (participant sees: Moral foundations: printed items) (p. 28)
  - Effect 5: Affect and risk (participant sees: Affect and risk, Certain outcome (kiss or $50): printed items; Affect and risk, 1% chance of the outcome: printed items) (p. 29)
  - Effect 6: Priming consumerism (participant sees: Priming consumerism, Consumer wording (“Consumer A”): printed items; Priming consumerism, Individual wording (“Individual A”): printed items) (p. 30)
  - Effect 7: Correspondence bias (participant sees: Correspondence bias, Pro-capital-punishment essay: printed items; Correspondence bias, Anti-capital-punishment essay: printed items) (p. 31, p. 32)
  - Effect 8: Disgust predicts homophobia (participant sees: Disgust predicts homophobia, Director encourages gay men to French kiss in public: printed items; Disgust predicts homophobia, Director encourages couples to French kiss in public: printed items) (p. 33)
  - Effect 9: Incidental anchors (participant sees: Incidental anchors, Phone named P97: printed items; Incidental anchors, Phone named P17: printed items) (p. 34, p. 35)
  - Effect 10: Social value orientations (participant sees: Social value orientations: printed items) (p. 36)
  - Effect 11: Trolley dilemma 1 (participant sees: Trolley dilemma 1, Foreseen side effect scenario (Denise, passenger on a train): printed items; Trolley dilemma 1, Greater good scenario (Frank, footbridge): printed items) (p. 37)
  - Effect 12: Sociometric status and well-being (participant sees: Sociometric status and well-being, Compare self with people on the very top rung of the local ladder (low sociometric status): printed items; Sociometric status and well-being, Compare self with people on the very bottom rung of the local ladder (high sociometric status): printed items) (p. 38)
  - Effect 13: False consensus: supermarket (participant sees: False consensus: supermarket: printed items) (p. 39)
- Individual difference measures in random order (p. 19, p. 17)
- Data quality items (p. 19)
- Demographics and debriefing (p. 17)

- **Result kind**: reported (p. 32)
- **Original finding** (verbatim, p. 31):
  > Miyamoto and Kitayama (2002) examined whether Americans would be more likely than Japanese to show a bias toward ascribing to an actor an attitude corresponding to the actor’s behavior, referred to as correspondence bias (Jones & Harris, 1967). ## Simulation scope

### Simulation scope, Slate 1

`sim-slate1`

- **Steps [implementation]**: A full session is a text survey: consent, effects in random order, individual differences, data quality items, demographics. An AI participant answers one page at a time. (p. 16)
- **Roles [implementation]**: Every participant is an AI participant; the study has no scripted roles. One run is one participant completing the whole slate. (p. 16)
- **Conditions [implementation]**: Runnable from the supplied US-English text: Structure and goal pursuit; Disfluency engages analytical processing; Moral foundations; Affect and risk; Priming consumerism; Correspondence bias; Disgust predicts homophobia; Incidental anchors; Social value orientations; Trolley dilemma 1; Sociometric status and well-being; False consensus: supermarket. Needs a vision-capable participant because the stimulus is a picture (supplied in the codebook): Direction and socioeconomic status. (p. 16)
- **Outcomes [implementation]**: The paper's own comparison per effect where the scoring is stated; effects needing covariates, multilevel models or unreported scoring return not_ready. (p. 16)
- **Limits [implementation]**: Only the US-English in-lab codebook is available: no translations, local currencies, site samples, lab-versus-online or WEIRD comparison, and no screen display, timing or click data. 125 samples across 36 countries cannot be reproduced by an AI participant. (p. 16, p. 15)

## Open issues

- **Participants per slate not reported (Slate 1)** [check, `iss-n-slate1`, p-slate1]: The paper reports 64 samples for the slate and a total of 15,305 participants, but not the participant count of the slate. The picture is supplied in the codebook, but the AI participants launched by the replay runner receive text only. Suggested: Decide whether to run this effect with a vision-capable participant (the package passes the picture files to such a participant) or to exclude it. (p. 23)
- **Visual manipulation in a text replay: Disfluency engages analytical processing** [decision, `iss-modal-alt`, m-alt-easy]: The disfluency manipulation is a visual font property that a text-only AI participant cannot perceive.
